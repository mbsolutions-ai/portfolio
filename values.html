Professional Values

A professional philosophy written for ALEC 451, the leadership model behind it, and a reflection on the course that changed how I think about communication.

Professional philosophy

Everything I do professionally comes back to a few commitments I made as an athlete long before I knew what I wanted to do for a living: show up prepared, tell the truth, do the boring work consistently, and treat the people around you as the reason the work matters.

Discipline is the foundation

I wake up at the same time, eat the same way, and train on the days I do not feel like it, because the days I do not feel like it are the ones that decide whether I am ready. That habit started with football and now runs through everything else. When I trade futures, the risk limit is set before I enter the position, not after. When I build a campaign, the audience research happens before the first post is written. Discipline is not about rigidity. It is what lets me stay calm when a plan meets reality, because I already did the work that the moment requires.

Honesty is the only currency that compounds

I want to be a trusted source of information, in a press release, in a client meeting, or in a bid that goes out under my company's name. My journalism coursework taught me that truth and transparency are not just ethical rules but the mechanism by which an audience decides to believe you. The beef-import analysis in this portfolio is really a case study in what happens when accurate information arrives too late and a false story fills the gap. I would rather say "I don't know yet" than say something I cannot stand behind. If I tell someone I will do something, it gets done.

A kicker cannot react to a miss, only to the next kick. The same is true of a bad meeting, a rejected draft, or a losing trade.

Leadership means raising the people around you

My model is transformational leadership, which I studied in LEAD 260 and recognized immediately from the best coaches and captains I have played for. It has four parts that I try to practice. Idealized influence: be the example, because people follow what you do before what you say. Inspirational motivation: start with why, so the team sees the point of the work and not just the task. Intellectual stimulation: invite better ideas, including ones that challenge mine. Individualized consideration: learn each person's strengths and give feedback specific enough to act on. The youth kicking camp I run is the clearest test of this. One precise correction, delivered right away, changes a kid's ball flight on the next rep, and watching that happen is better than any kick I have made myself.

Two men I interviewed for coursework sharpened this. Karl Royston, a former nuclear submarine officer, told me he practices servant leadership: care more about your team's success than your own, and remove the obstacles in their way. Mike Miskho, a director at Analog Devices, told me to "lead by listening." Both of them run teams I would want to be on.

Faith and purpose

My faith gives me a sense of purpose that makes the sacrifices worthwhile and keeps me honest with myself about my decisions. It shows up in how I try to treat people: with respect, with loyalty, and with the assumption that they deserve my best effort whether or not anyone is keeping score.

What I am still working on

Self-awareness is a strength; empathy is the growth area. I am good at controlling my own emotions under pressure and less practiced at reading other people's, and I can let a negative attitude in the room pull my own mood down. I am also better at making a message than proving it worked, so the skill I am building now is working with data: owning the numbers on my projects and building the reports myself instead of handing them off. A professional who can communicate and measure is worth more than one who can only do one.

Values I work from

  • Discipline. Same routine on hard days as easy ones. The plan is set before the pressure arrives.
  • Honesty and integrity. Be the person people can rely on to tell the truth, even when it costs something.
  • Respect. Every person and every idea gets a fair hearing. It keeps me from putting my own agenda above the audience's needs.
  • Faith. Purpose beyond the scoreboard, and a reason to keep my word.
  • Continuous improvement. Critique the wins as hard as the losses. Seek feedback on purpose, not by accident.

Leadership model: four practices

  • Idealized influence — be the example.
  • Inspirational motivation — start with why.
  • Intellectual stimulation — invite better ideas.
  • Individualized consideration — specific feedback, person by person.

Adapted from my LEAD 260 leadership story, which also set two growth goals I still keep: seek feedback from mentors and teammates on a schedule, and build professional relationships on purpose rather than by chance.

Coursework reflection: AGCM 420, Public Information Campaigns

If one course changed how I understand my own degree, it was AGCM 420. I came in thinking a public information campaign was about presenting facts. Working with Midwest Dairy on "Life on the Moo-ve" taught me it is about influence: understanding what the audience believes, choosing the channels they already trust, and finding a story they will actually share. Thinking through how a dairy calf would "tell her story" on TikTok forced decisions about tone, length, visuals, and audience expectations that I had never had to make before.

Meeting with Alyson Fendrick at Midwest Dairy twice, and revising our plan around her feedback, gave the project a sense of professional accountability that no graded assignment could. Her suggestion to add YouTube for long-form content changed our platform strategy and taught me to listen to stakeholders before defending a plan. The group work mattered too: we divided responsibilities by strength, I owned the mediums and storyboards, and I learned that keeping a team on track and hitting outside deadlines is a skill in its own right.

What I carry forward is simple. Audience, medium, content, context. Every campaign, every product pitch, and every crisis response in this portfolio runs on that framework, and it came from this course.
